SLIDE 1
My thesis investigates how color constancy can assist monocular depth estimation. This presentation reports the first implemented experiment. I ran a pretrained depth model and controlled color transformations on real RGB images with reference depth. The results are a local pilot, not a full benchmark reproduction or a new trained model. SLIDE 2
Monocular depth estimation predicts the distance of each pixel from one RGB image. Color constancy estimates or compensates for illumination color. Here I test a concrete question: if I change the input color without changing the geometry, how does the predicted depth change? Then I test whether simple correction reduces the measured error. These are synthetic global casts of real photographs. They do not represent every real lighting or camera condition.

SLIDE 3
The system has four stages. First, it loads aligned RGB and depth data. Second, it creates seven image conditions: the original, two warm casts, two cool casts, and two green casts. Each condition uses four processing choices, which gives 28 predictions per image. Third, the same pretrained model predicts depth for every input. Finally, the evaluator uses an identical crop, mask, and depth range. I save depth arrays, transformation metadata, runtime, and scores. No model training occurred.

This is a small deterministic pilot using real NYU Depth V2 images with aligned reference depth. Each evaluation image represents a distinct scene. Development and evaluation scenes are separate. The checkpoint predicts metric depth directly. I do not fit a scale or shift to the reference depth. SLIDE 5
I compare no correction with three fixed correction baselines. Gray-World assumes that the scene-average channel values provide useful information about the illuminant. White-Patch uses the channel maxima. Both compute inverse gains normalized with the geometric mean. The half-strength variant takes the square root of the Gray-World gains. It is a fixed ablation, not a novelty claim or a setting selected on evaluation ground truth. I convert sRGB to linear RGB before these operations, preserve mean luminance before clipping, clip the values, and convert back to sRGB. Clipping is recorded because it can remove information and make correction irreversible.

Measured source: results/summary.json, results/metrics.csv, data/nyu_pilot/manifest.json

SLIDE 6
This table establishes the clean-input baseline and checks the effect of applying correction when no synthetic cast is present. The no-correction model has mean AbsRel 0.2460, RMSE 0.7961 meters and delta-one accuracy 63.09%. AbsRel is absolute depth error divided by reference depth, averaged over valid pixels. RMSE is root mean square depth error in meters. Delta-one is the fraction of valid pixels where the larger of prediction divided by reference and reference divided by prediction is below 1.25. These are means of per-image scores on this subset. They should not be compared directly with another paper unless the dataset split and all evaluation settings match.

SLIDE 8
I average the six cast conditions for each method and each image. Gray-World has the lowest corrupted-input AbsRel among the three fixed correction baselines in this pilot: 0.2409, compared with 0.2394 without correction. This corresponds to a 0.63 percent increase in average AbsRel. This is a descriptive, exploratory comparison. I did not tune a model or establish a new algorithm. I also make a paired comparison per image, keeping its six casts together. The mean reduction is -0.00150 with a 95 percent bootstrap interval from -0.00613 to 0.00254. A positive reduction means correction helped. A negative reduction means it harmed. I resample images 10,000 times. 19 images improve and 13 worsen. This interval describes the sampled images and does not establish generalization to other datasets or all real lighting.

SLIDE 10
I also inspect the image with the weakest average Gray-World effect across the six casts. Image nyu_01353, condition green_strong. Extreme image by mean six-cast Gray-World AbsRel reduction; displayed cast is least favorable within that image. In the displayed comparison, Gray-World increases AbsRel by 0.0730. The mean six-cast AbsRel reduction for this image is -0.0411. This is a selected extreme and does not represent the typical result. A global illuminant estimator can confuse the color of the scene with the color of the light. A scene dominated by one surface color can violate Gray-World assumptions. Channel maxima can also make White-Patch unstable. Clipping can discard color information before correction. These are possible mechanisms to investigate with further controlled tests, not a proven causal diagnosis of this single example. The clean-input comparison and per-image paired results show why applying correction to every input needs further study.

SLIDE 12
The current progress is a working system and a controlled first experiment. This descriptive pilot suggests what to investigate next. Compared with no correction, the lowest-error correction baseline has 0.63 percent higher average cast AbsRel, and 1.73 percent lower clean-input AbsRel. Both comparisons use the same fixed model and evaluation images. Neither result justifies a universal claim about color correction or a novel-method claim. My next steps are a larger fixed subset, another depth backbone, a separate clipping control, and images captured under real illumination changes. Only after these checks should I decide whether an adaptive correction method is justified. I would welcome the supervisor's guidance on the most useful next comparison.
